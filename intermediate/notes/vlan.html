VLAN Notes

The Problem: Life Before VLANs

Imagine a traditional network where all the computers, printers, and servers are plugged into a single physical switch. This entire setup creates one massive broadcast domain.

Whenever a device needs to find another device, such as sending an ARP request, it shouts a broadcast message to everyone on that switch.

The result: everyone has to stop and listen to that noise. As the network grows, this constant shouting clogs up the network and degrades performance.

The Solution: The Virtual Switch

802.1Q is where the Virtual Local Area Network, or VLAN, steps in. Instead of buying separate, expensive physical switches for HR, IT, and Sales, a VLAN allows you to take one physical switch and logically slice it into multiple virtual switches.

Security Risks Without Segmentation

Insider threats: If everyone is in the same domain, an employee in Sales running a packet sniffer can much more easily capture sensitive traffic meant for HR.

Malware spread: Without internal boundaries, a single infected computer can spread worms or ransomware across the entire company network unchecked.

Performance loss: The main performance killer is the risk of a broadcast storm. If too many devices broadcast at once, or if a loop is created in the wiring, the switch gets overwhelmed and the whole network can crash.

Important Adjustment

VLAN hopping is an attack that specifically targets VLAN configurations. Threat actors cannot do it in the "before VLANs" scenario, but it is exactly what they may try after VLANs are implemented. A later security chapter should cover how to prevent it.

How It Works Under the Hood

Now we know we need to separate HR, such as VLAN 10, and Sales, such as VLAN 20, even though they are plugged into the exact same physical switch.

When a standard computer in HR sends out data, it has no idea what a VLAN is. As far as the computer knows, it is just sending a standard Ethernet frame. The magic happens the moment that data enters the switch.
