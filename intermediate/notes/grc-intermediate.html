GRC Intermediate Guide

Why this chapter matters

GRC turns security knowledge into accountable decisions.

At this level, the challenge is no longer defining risk. It is building processes that people can actually follow, producing evidence that can be trusted, and helping owners make informed decisions when security is imperfect.

How to read this guide

Follow the stages in order. Pause at the examples, explain the idea in your own words, and complete the practice sections before moving forward.

Stage 1 of 6

Think and Write Like a GRC Professional

Translate technical facts into clear risk statements, ratings, ownership, and treatment decisions that a business can act on.

1. Intermediate GRC Mindset

At foundation level, you learn terms.

At intermediate level, you start operating like a professional.

A professional GRC person does not only ask:

"Are we compliant?"

They ask:

"What are our most important risks, how well are our controls working, where is the evidence, who owns the decision, and what should leadership do next?"

Intermediate GRC requires balance.

You must understand:

  • Technical security
  • Business priorities
  • Legal and regulatory expectations
  • Audit evidence
  • Risk language
  • Human behavior
  • Practical constraints

In real companies, security is rarely perfect.

Your job is to help the company make informed decisions.

2. How to Think Like a GRC Professional

Weak Thinking

"This system is vulnerable. Fix it."

Better Thinking

"This vulnerability affects an internet-facing system that stores customer data. The likelihood is high because exploitation is possible remotely. The impact is high due to potential data exposure. This should be treated as a high risk and remediated within the patch SLA."

Senior Direction

"This risk should be prioritized because it affects a critical business service, could breach customer contract requirements, and may create regulatory and reputational impact. If remediation is delayed, leadership should formally accept the residual risk with a compensating control."

3. Writing Professional Risk Statements

A good risk statement usually includes:

  • Cause
  • Event
  • Impact

Formula:

Due to [weakness], there is a risk that [event] may occur, resulting in [business impact].

Example 1: Missing MFA

Due to lack of MFA on privileged accounts, there is a risk that attackers may gain unauthorized administrative access, resulting in data exposure, system disruption, or unauthorized changes.

Example 2: No Vendor Review

Due to absence of vendor security assessments, there is a risk that third-party providers may expose company data or services, resulting in data breach, service disruption, or contractual non-compliance.

Example 3: Weak Logging

Due to insufficient logging and monitoring, there is a risk that security incidents may not be detected or investigated in a timely manner, resulting in prolonged attacker access and increased business impact.

5. Risk Rating in Practice

Many companies use a simple 3x3 or 5x5 matrix.

3x3 Risk Matrix

Impact / Likelihood Low Medium High
High Medium High Critical
Medium Low Medium High
Low Low Low Medium

Likelihood Factors

Consider:

  • Is the system internet-facing?
  • Is the vulnerability easy to exploit?
  • Are attackers actively exploiting this?
  • Are there existing controls?
  • Has this happened before?
  • Is the user group high-risk?

Impact Factors

Consider:

  • Customer data exposure
  • Financial loss
  • Legal or regulatory impact
  • Operational downtime
  • Reputation damage
  • Contractual penalties
  • Safety impact
  • Executive concern

Stage 2 of 6

Operate and Test Controls

Learn how control design, operating effectiveness, access reviews, and joiner-mover-leaver processes work in practice.

6. Control Design vs Control Operating Effectiveness

This is very important.

Control Design

Is the control designed properly?

Example:

Company requires MFA only for normal users but not administrators.

Design issue:

The control is poorly designed because privileged accounts are higher risk.

Control Operating Effectiveness

Is the control actually working over time?

Example:

MFA policy exists, but 20 admin accounts do not have MFA enabled.

Operating issue:

The control is not operating effectively.

Professional Language

Instead of saying:

"MFA is broken."

Say:

"The MFA control is not operating effectively because privileged account coverage is incomplete."

7. Control Testing

Control testing verifies whether a control works.

Example Control

User access must be reviewed quarterly.

Test Steps

  1. Obtain list of active users.
  2. Obtain previous quarter access review evidence.
  3. Check if review was completed.
  4. Check if reviewer was appropriate.
  5. Check if exceptions were tracked.
  6. Check if access removals were completed.
  7. Document result.

Possible Result

Control partially effective. Quarterly access review was completed, but evidence did not show whether identified removals were performed.

8. Access Review

Access review is one of the most common GRC activities.

Purpose:

Ensure users have only the access they need.

What to Review

  • Active users
  • Privileged users
  • Shared accounts
  • Dormant accounts
  • Former employees
  • Third-party accounts
  • Admin group membership
  • Critical application roles

Evidence Needed

  • User access export
  • Reviewer approval
  • List of removed users
  • Tickets showing changes
  • Date of review
  • Sign-off

Red Flags

  • Former employees still active
  • Admin access without approval
  • Shared admin accounts
  • No business owner review
  • No evidence of removals
  • Review done by IT only without business owner validation

9. Joiner, Mover, Leaver Process

This is also called JML.

Joiner

New employee joins.

Controls:

  • Access request approved
  • Role-based access assigned
  • MFA enabled
  • Security training completed

Mover

Employee changes role.

Controls:

  • Old access removed
  • New access approved
  • Privileged access reviewed

Leaver

Employee leaves.

Controls:

  • Account disabled quickly
  • VPN/email/app access removed
  • Assets returned
  • Shared passwords changed if needed
  • Sessions revoked

Risk Example

Due to weak leaver process, former employees may retain access to company systems, resulting in unauthorized access or data theft.

10. Vendor Risk Management

Vendor risk is extremely important for modern companies.

Vendors may access:

  • Customer data
  • Internal systems
  • Cloud platforms
  • Source code
  • Financial information
  • Employee data

Vendor Risk Questions

Ask:

  • What data will the vendor access?
  • Is the vendor critical to operations?
  • Where is data stored?
  • Does the vendor have security certifications?
  • Does the vendor use encryption?
  • Does the vendor have incident response procedures?
  • Does the contract include security requirements?
  • Can the vendor subcontract work?
  • What happens if the vendor is breached?

Vendor Risk Tiers

Tier Example Review Depth
High Cloud provider, payment processor, payroll vendor Full review
Medium HR software, ticketing tool Moderate review
Low Public newsletter tool Basic review

Evidence to Request

  • Security questionnaire
  • ISO 27001 certificate
  • SOC 2 report
  • Penetration test summary
  • Data processing agreement
  • Incident response policy
  • Business continuity plan
  • Access control policy

11. Audit Readiness

Audit readiness means being prepared before auditors ask.

Common Audit Evidence

  • Approved policies
  • Risk register
  • Asset inventory
  • Access reviews
  • Vulnerability reports
  • Patch reports
  • Incident records
  • Backup test reports
  • Security training records
  • Vendor assessments
  • Change management tickets

Good Audit Behavior

  • Be honest.
  • Provide only requested evidence.
  • Do not guess.
  • Do not hide known issues.
  • Track evidence clearly.
  • Keep timestamps and owners.
  • Explain compensating controls if something is missing.

Bad Audit Behavior

  • Sending random screenshots
  • Changing evidence after request
  • Giving verbal answers only
  • Over-sharing unrelated issues
  • Blaming teams
  • Claiming controls work without proof

12. Evidence Management

Evidence should answer:

What control was performed, when, by whom, and what was the result?

Good Evidence Example

For patch management:

  • Vulnerability scan report dated March 31
  • Patch deployment report dated April 5
  • Exception list for systems not patched
  • Approval for exception
  • Retest report showing vulnerabilities fixed

Poor Evidence Example

  • Screenshot of dashboard with no date
  • Verbal statement: "We patch monthly"
  • Old report from last year
  • No system list
  • No exception tracking

13. Policy Management

Policies should not be written and forgotten.

Policy Lifecycle

  1. Draft
  2. Review
  3. Approve
  4. Publish
  5. Communicate
  6. Train
  7. Review periodically
  8. Update when needed

Important Policies

Small and medium companies should usually have:

  • Information Security Policy
  • Access Control Policy
  • Password/MFA Policy
  • Acceptable Use Policy
  • Incident Response Policy
  • Data Classification Policy
  • Backup Policy
  • Vendor Security Policy
  • Change Management Policy
  • Asset Management Policy

Policy Quality Checklist

A good policy should define:

  • Purpose
  • Scope
  • Roles and responsibilities
  • Requirements
  • Exceptions
  • Enforcement
  • Review cycle
  • Approval owner

14. Exception Management

Real companies always have exceptions.

Example:

A legacy system does not support MFA.

Bad approach:

Ignore it.

Professional approach:

  • Document the exception.
  • Explain the risk.
  • Define compensating controls.
  • Get owner approval.
  • Set expiry date.
  • Review regularly.

Exception Template

Field Example
Exception ID EX-001
Control MFA required for all admin access
Exception Legacy firewall does not support MFA
Risk Unauthorized admin access
Compensating Control VPN restriction, IP allowlist, strong password, logging
Owner Network Manager
Expiry 90 days
Approval CISO/IT Head

15. GRC and Vulnerability Management

A technical scanner may say:

Critical vulnerability found.

GRC asks:

  • Which asset is affected?
  • Is it internet-facing?
  • Is it business critical?
  • Is exploit available?
  • What is the patch SLA?
  • Who owns remediation?
  • Is there an exception?
  • Is risk accepted?
  • Was remediation verified?

Patch SLA Example

Severity Internal System Internet-Facing System
Critical 15 days 7 days
High 30 days 15 days
Medium 60 days 30 days
Low 90 days 60 days

16. Incident Response from GRC View

SOC focuses on detection and response.

GRC focuses on process, evidence, and improvement.

GRC Questions After an Incident

  • Was the incident identified quickly?
  • Was it classified correctly?
  • Was escalation followed?
  • Were stakeholders notified?
  • Was evidence preserved?
  • Was root cause identified?
  • Were lessons learned documented?
  • Were policies or controls updated?
  • Was customer/regulatory notification needed?

Incident Evidence

  • Alert records
  • Timeline
  • Investigation notes
  • Affected assets
  • Containment actions
  • Recovery actions
  • Communication records
  • Lessons learned report
  • Post-incident action items

17. Business Continuity and Disaster Recovery

Business Continuity

How the business continues during disruption.

Example:

How does the company operate if the office is unavailable?

Disaster Recovery

How IT systems are restored.

Example:

How do we restore the database after ransomware?

Important Terms

Term Meaning
RTO Recovery Time Objective: how fast to restore
RPO Recovery Point Objective: acceptable data loss
BIA Business Impact Analysis
DR Test Test of recovery process

Example

If a payment system has:

  • RTO: 4 hours
  • RPO: 15 minutes

Then the company expects:

  • System restored within 4 hours
  • No more than 15 minutes of data lost

18. Data Classification

Data classification helps decide protection level.

Simple Classification Model

Level Example Protection
Public Marketing brochure Low
Internal Internal process docs Medium
Confidential Employee records High
Restricted Passwords, financial records, customer secrets Very High

Controls by Classification

Restricted data may require:

  • Encryption
  • MFA
  • Access approval
  • Logging
  • DLP
  • Retention limits
  • Secure deletion
  • Strong vendor controls

19. Privacy Basics

Privacy focuses on personal data.

Personal data may include:

  • Name
  • Email
  • Phone number
  • Address
  • Citizenship number
  • Financial information
  • Health information
  • Location data
  • Employee records

Privacy Principles

  • Collect only needed data.
  • Use data for clear purpose.
  • Protect data.
  • Limit access.
  • Do not retain forever.
  • Delete securely.
  • Notify users when required.
  • Review vendors handling personal data.

GRC Privacy Questions

  • What personal data do we collect?
  • Why do we collect it?
  • Where is it stored?
  • Who can access it?
  • How long do we keep it?
  • Which vendors process it?
  • What happens if it is breached?

20. Framework Mapping

Framework mapping means connecting controls from one standard to another.

Example:

Control Area ISO 27001 NIST CSF Practical Control
Access Control Access management Protect MFA and least privilege
Logging Monitoring Detect SIEM alerts
Incident Response Incident management Respond IR plan and playbooks
Backups Continuity Recover Backup and restore testing

Mapping is useful because one control can satisfy multiple requirements.

Example:

Quarterly access review may support ISO 27001, SOC 2, internal policy, and customer requirements.

21. GRC for Nepali Companies

In Nepal, many companies may not have mature GRC teams.

You may see:

  • Informal access processes
  • Limited documentation
  • Manual evidence collection
  • Weak vendor review
  • Policies copied from templates
  • Security controlled mostly by IT
  • Compliance driven by clients or regulators

This is normal in growing markets.

Your value is helping the company mature step by step.

Practical Priorities

For small and medium companies, start with:

  1. Asset inventory
  2. User access review
  3. MFA for critical systems
  4. Backup and restore testing
  5. Incident response plan
  6. Security policies
  7. Vendor list and basic risk review
  8. Vulnerability management
  9. Security awareness training
  10. Risk register

For IT Outsourcing Companies

Focus on:

  • Customer data protection
  • Developer access control
  • Source code security
  • Cloud security
  • Laptop security
  • Employee offboarding
  • Client contract requirements
  • Secure SDLC
  • Evidence for international clients

For Banks and Fintechs

Focus on:

  • Strong access controls

  • Audit logging
  • Regulatory reporting
  • Vendor risk

  • Disaster recovery
  • Incident response

22. Communication Skills

GRC is communication-heavy.

You must talk to:

  • IT
  • SOC
  • Developers
  • HR
  • Legal
  • Finance
  • Vendors
  • Auditors
  • Executives
  • Business owners

Technical Message

"Port 3389 is exposed to the internet."

GRC Message

"Remote Desktop exposure increases the risk of unauthorized access and ransomware. This should be restricted through VPN or IP allowlisting."

Executive Message

"This issue increases the likelihood of business disruption. We recommend fixing it within 7 days or formally accepting the risk."

23. Reporting to Management

A good management report should be simple.

Monthly GRC Report Sections

  • Top risks
  • New risks
  • Closed risks
  • Overdue remediation
  • Audit findings
  • Vendor review status
  • Security exceptions
  • Incident trends
  • Policy updates
  • Key decisions needed

Example Executive Summary

During this month, three high risks were identified: missing MFA on privileged accounts, incomplete vendor assessments, and delayed critical patching. MFA remediation is in progress and expected to close by month-end. Vendor assessments remain overdue and require business owner support. No critical incidents were reported.

24. Metrics and KRIs

Metrics help show whether security is improving.

Useful Metrics

Metric Meaning
% users with MFA Access security maturity
# overdue high risks Risk management health
% critical patches within SLA Vulnerability management
# vendors reviewed Third-party risk coverage
# failed access reviews Access governance issue
Mean time to close audit findings Remediation performance
# security exceptions expired Governance weakness

KRI vs KPI

KPI measures performance.

Example:

95% of employees completed training.

KRI measures risk.

Example:

20 privileged users do not have MFA.

25. How to Apply GRC in Real Work

When you find an issue, follow this process:

  1. Understand the issue.
  2. Identify affected asset or process.
  3. Identify business impact.
  4. Write a risk statement.
  5. Check existing controls.
  6. Rate likelihood and impact.
  7. Recommend treatment.
  8. Assign owner.
  9. Define due date.
  10. Collect evidence.
  11. Track progress.
  12. Report status.
  13. Verify closure.

Example

Finding:

Developers share one production database account.

Professional handling:

Step Action
Issue Shared production DB account
Risk No accountability and possible unauthorized changes
Impact Data manipulation, breach, audit failure
Control Individual named accounts, least privilege, logging
Owner Engineering Manager
Evidence User list, DB permissions, ticket approvals
Due Date 30 days
Status Open

26. Intermediate Interview Questions

Question 1

How do you test whether access review is effective?

Good answer:

I would obtain the user access list, review evidence, approval records, and removal tickets. I would check whether the review was done on time, by the correct owner, whether exceptions were identified, and whether access removals were completed.

Question 2

What is the difference between inherent and residual risk?

Good answer:

Inherent risk is the risk before controls. Residual risk is the remaining risk after controls are applied.

Question 3

How would you handle a vendor with no SOC 2 report or ISO/IEC 27001 certification?

Good answer:

I would not automatically reject them. I would assess the type of data and access involved, send a security questionnaire, review their policies and controls, identify gaps, require compensating controls if needed, and escalate high risks to the business owner.

Question 4

What makes good audit evidence?

Good answer:

Good evidence is relevant, complete, dated, traceable, and shows that the control operated as required.

Question 5

How would you explain a critical vulnerability to management?

Good answer:

I would explain which business system is affected, what could happen if exploited, whether exploitation is likely, what the remediation plan is, who owns it, and when it should be fixed.

27. Common Mistakes at Intermediate Level

Avoid these mistakes:

  • Treating compliance as checkbox work only
  • Using too much technical language with executives
  • Accepting screenshots as evidence without context
  • Not assigning risk owners
  • Not tracking exceptions
  • Confusing vulnerability severity with business risk
  • Assuming policy means control is working
  • Ignoring vendor risk
  • Reporting too many details and no decision points
  • Saying "secure" or "not secure" without explaining risk

30. What Senior GRC Professionals Do Differently

A junior says:

"This control failed."

An intermediate says:

"This control failed and should be remediated."

A senior says:

"This control failed because ownership and process design are weak. The risk affects customer trust and regulatory exposure. We need a sustainable operating model, not a one-time fix."

Senior professionals think in systems.

They ask:

  • Why did the control fail?
  • Is this isolated or systemic?
  • Is ownership clear?
  • Is the process scalable?
  • Is the evidence reliable?
  • Is the risk accepted by the right person?
  • Is management seeing the right information?
  • Are we reducing real risk or only passing audits?

33. Final Intermediate Summary

Intermediate GRC is about applying structure to security problems.

You should be able to:

  • Identify and write risks clearly
  • Build and maintain a risk register
  • Test controls
  • Collect strong evidence
  • Prepare for audits
  • Review vendors
  • Track exceptions
  • Communicate with business owners
  • Translate technical issues into business risk
  • Support compliance without becoming a checkbox person

The core professional habit:

Always connect technical facts to business risk, ownership, evidence, and decision-making.

This is the bridge from foundation to professional GRC.
